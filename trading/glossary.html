Reference

Glossary

Every term used in the dashboard and playbook, in plain English.
CAGR
Compound annual growth rate — what an investment actually earned per year once compounding is accounted for. The destination number.
Sharpe ratio
Average return divided by volatility (annualized). Return per unit of stress. Roughly: 0.5 mediocre, 0.8 decent, 1.0+ good for simple rules.
Drawdown / max drawdown
Drawdown: how far below your own prior peak you currently sit. Max drawdown: the worst such fall in the whole record. The journey number — it decides whether you'd actually have held on.
Volatility
How much returns swing, usually annualized standard deviation. ~15% is normal for stocks; 30%+ is storm territory.
Beta
How much a stock moves per 1% market move. Beta 2 ≈ doubles every market swing, both directions.
Correlation
How much two things move together (−1 to +1). Low-correlation assets are diversifiers; in crashes, most correlations lurch toward 1.
Regime
A persistent market 'mood' — calm, choppy, stressed. Regimes cluster, which is why risk is more predictable than return.
Hidden Markov model
A statistical model that assumes an unobservable state generates the data, and infers today's state from recent behavior. Our regime detector.
Walk-forward
Testing discipline where the model only ever uses data available at each historical moment. The difference between a backtest and a fairy tale.
Lookahead bias
Accidentally letting a backtest peek at the future (e.g., using a month's average price to trade that same month). The most common way backtests lie.
Credit spread / OAS
Extra yield lenders demand from risky borrowers over Treasuries. Widening spreads = 'borrowing is getting harder' = the classic pre-storm signal.
10-month moving average
Average of the last ~10 months of prices. Price above it = uptrend intact. Old (1930s), simple, and still the benchmark trend filter.
Base rate
What happens on average, ignoring all signals — here, stocks up ~64% of months, +1.04% (~1%) per month. Every trade idea must beat this, not zero.
t-statistic
How many standard errors an effect is from zero. |t| > 2 is the conventional 'probably real' bar. Small samples almost never clear it — respect that.
Hit rate
Percent of months a condition saw positive returns. 64% base rate is why shorting is structurally hard.
Conjunction
Two signals required together (credit stress AND broken trend). Conjunctions are where real edges usually hide — single indicators are too crowded.
Drawdown insurance
Giving up expected return to shrink worst-case losses (what trend/regime filters actually do). Insurance costs premium; the premium here is ~2.4% CAGR.
Equity risk premium
Expected stock return minus safe bond yield — the payment for bearing risk. Near zero today, which is what 'expensive market' means in one number.
Transition matrix
The regime model's table of switching odds: given today's mood, the probability of each mood tomorrow. Big diagonal numbers = moods are sticky = risk is forecastable.
Emission
What a hidden state 'shows' you — the daily return and volatility a regime generates. You infer the hidden mood from its visible emissions.
Baum–Welch (EM)
The fitting loop for a hidden Markov model: guess the states, sort days into them, re-measure each state, repeat until stable. No hand labels anywhere.
Forward algorithm
The daily Bayes update that turns yesterday's regime probabilities plus today's market move into today's regime probabilities. Cheap, online, no peeking ahead.
Correlation network
A graph where stocks are dots and strong return correlations are lines. The market's real seating chart — who actually trades together, whatever the sector label says.
Label propagation
Cluster discovery by peer pressure: every stock repeatedly adopts the most common group among its correlated neighbors until the map settles.
Long / flat / short
The three positions: own it, don't own it, bet against it. Long needs no forecast (drift pays you), flat needs a risk signal, short needs a direction edge — the rarest thing in markets.
Cluster migration
A stock changing which group it trades with — often the market repricing its story before the news says why. Worth investigating every time.
Prior / likelihood / posterior
The three beats of a Bayes update: what you believed this morning (prior), how well each possibility explains what you just saw (likelihood), what you believe now (posterior = prior × likelihood, rescaled).
Attention effect
Barber–Odean: retail investors buy whatever grabbed their attention; the surge overshoots, and returns after attention spikes are below average. Spikes are closer to sell signals.
Crowding
How one-sided the positioning in a trade has become. Crowded trades aren't wrong — they're fragile: everyone heads for the same exit at once.
Alternative data
Anything that estimates fundamentals before they're reported: card panels, satellite images, app downloads, scraped sentiment. The modern arms race in one phrase.
S&P 500 / SPY / VOO
The S&P 500 is an index of the 500 largest US public companies — the default meaning of 'the market' on this site. SPY and VOO are funds that each hold all 500, so owning either is owning a slice of corporate America. We treat them as one position.
Index fund
A fund that automatically buys every stock in an index instead of picking favorites. Cheap, diversified, and the benchmark every clever strategy here must justify itself against.
Annualized
A rate rescaled to per-year so periods of different lengths can be compared. '11% annualized volatility' doesn't mean 11% happened — it means at the current pace, a year would swing that much.
Bond
A loan to a company or government that pays fixed interest. The lender's best case is merely getting repaid — which is why bond investors are professionally paranoid, and why they smell trouble first.
Treasuries
Bonds issued by the US government — treated as the 'risk-free' baseline that every other investment gets priced against.
Yield
The effective interest rate a bond pays at its current price. Price and yield move opposite ways: pay less for the same payments, earn more.
High-yield ('junk') bonds
Bonds from risky borrowers, who must pay extra yield to attract lenders. That extra yield over Treasuries is the credit spread — this system's smoke detector.
CAPE (Shiller CAPE)
Price divided by ten years of inflation-adjusted average earnings — the long-run gauge of how expensive the market is. Readings above ~40 (today's zone) have only occurred near major tops.
Standard deviation (σ)
The typical distance of outcomes from their average. A 1σ day is ordinary, 3σ is rare, and 4.5σ means 'your definition of normal is being violated.'
Z-score
How many standard deviations an observation sits from its average — the universal 'how weird is this?' unit.
Bell curve (normal distribution)
The shape where most outcomes cluster near the average and extremes fall away fast. Each regime in our model is a bell curve over daily returns with its own width.
Option / put
An option is a contract to buy or sell at a fixed price by a fixed date. A put pays off when prices fall — buying one is crash insurance with a known maximum cost, unlike shorting, where losses are unbounded.
Markov property
Tomorrow depends only on today's state, not the deeper past — like weather: a stormy day predicts another stormy day, regardless of how long ago summer was.
Expanding window
Refitting a model on all history up to each date — the training set grows over time and never contains the future. The stricter cousin of walk-forward.
P&L
Profit and loss — what a position or strategy made or lost over some period.
Discounting
Future money is worth less than money today, so asset prices shrink expected future cash flows back to the present. When interest rates rise, the shrink is harsher — hitting hardest the assets whose payoffs sit furthest in the future.
Risk-on / risk-off / exposure
Risk-on: own stocks. Risk-off (de-risk): own fewer or none. Exposure: the share of your money currently in stocks — the main dial this whole system turns.
Half-life
How long an effect takes to fade to half its strength. Viral sentiment: days. Market regimes: months.
Mean reversion
The strategy family that buys panic and sells recovery — betting stretched prices snap back. Wins small and often; loses huge in real crashes. The mirror image of trend following.
Breakout
Buying when price exceeds its recent range high, betting a new trend is starting. Works on trending instruments (commodities, FX); fails on choppy ones (stock indexes).
Pairs trading / stat-arb
Betting the gap between two related assets (Coke vs Pepsi) closes when it stretches unusually wide. Direction-neutral — you trade the relationship, not the market.
Carry
Being paid simply to hold: coupons, dividends, sold-option premium. Steady income that hides its risk until the insured event arrives — 'nickels in front of a steamroller.'
Risk premium
Payment earned for holding something uncomfortable that others want off their books. The honest answer to 'why does this strategy make money' for most strategies that actually do.
Forced seller
Someone who must sell now regardless of price — margin calls, fund redemptions, stop-losses. The person on the other side of the mean-reversion trade, and the reason it pays.
